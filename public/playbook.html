Weather
Check the market first
How strong the market is sets how hard you push
What this is
Before I look at a single chart, I check the market as a whole.
The measure is breadth. Breadth counts how many stocks made a big up-move today against how many made a big down-move. That gives me one of three answers: the market is strong, mixed, or weak.
Most stocks move with the market. So this is the first question of my day, not the last.
Why it matters
One question decides how I play the whole day. Is this a market where breakouts work, or one where they get sold?
This check never picks my stocks. It sets two things: how big I trade, and how patient I am.
The same setup, in the same stock, can be a full-size trade one week and a pass the next. Nothing about the chart changed. The market did.
How I read it
I take the daily count of big up-moves against big down-moves, and I compare them over two periods: the month and the quarter. Three readings matter.
—
Both green — buyers outnumber sellers on both periods. Breakouts are more likely to work. This is when I push.
—
A sudden burst of big gainers after a weak stretch. This often marks a turn. It is why I check every day instead of once a week.
—
A flood of heavy down-moves. On the daily board that is a warning. On the quarterly board, a very low up-count is the panic reading, and it has marked major lows.
What I do with each answer
—
Strong — the full playbook.
—
Mixed — half size, take profit sooner, only the best setups.
—
Weak — the best work I can do is build the watchlist for when it turns.
I check once, before I open the watchlist, and that answer stands for the day. Everything else in this book assumes I have done it.
Common mistakes
Trading the market you wish you had instead of the one in front of you. The most tempting version is buying the first green day after heavy selling. It feels like the turn. Most of the time it fades by lunch. Wait for the market to confirm.
Checking after you enter instead of before. That is the same mistake, just later.
Takeaway
Market first, setup second, size third. If the market check is not green, do not push.

Weather
Markets move in groups, themes and sectors
Money flows into a whole group at once, not one stock at a time
What this is
Big money does not buy one stock at a time. It buys a whole group at once: chip makers, banks, uranium, whatever is being funded that month.
You will hear these called groups, themes or sectors. They mean the same thing. This book says group throughout.
Every stock belongs to a group, and that group is either being bought or it is not. When a stock's group is near the top of my performance lists, I call that stock in-theme. It is a status I look up, not a feeling I have.
Why it matters
About half of any stock's move comes from its group, not from the stock itself.
So when a group is being bought, even its third-best chart tends to beat the best chart in a group being sold.
That means the group tells me as much about the outcome as the chart does. And I can look it up before I enter.
How I read it
I track how every group performed over the past week and the past month, and I mark the top five on each list. A stock whose group is on those lists is in-theme.
The rule is written down on purpose, so a good-looking chart cannot talk me out of it.
Reading the two lists together
—
On both lists — real leadership. This is my main hunting ground.
—
Strong this week, absent on the month — new money arriving. I watch for first bases.
—
Strong month, fading week — a leader resting, or a leader ending. The charts of its top names tell me which.
—
Today's big gainers from the month's worst groups — usually just a bounce in something beaten down, not the start of leadership.
What I do with it
In-theme stocks get the full playbook.
If the group is on neither list, the bar goes up. The setup has to be excellent, the size is smaller, and I give it less room.
I check the group before I look at the chart.
Common mistakes
Staying in love with one stock while its whole group turns down.
Ignoring the rule because the story is exciting. If the story were real, the group would already show it.
Deciding a stock's group by instinct. Which group a stock trades with is something you look up. Never something you decide for yourself.
Takeaway
Strong stocks live in strong groups. Check the group before the chart, and let the rule say no for you.

Weather
Rotation
Where the money is moving now — and the bounce that looks like leadership
What this is
Rotation is money moving from one group to another.
Between the whole market and the single stock sits the group table. Every group is ranked against its own recent history, with a second, faster reading for this week.
Strong on both means the group is leading and speeding up. Those are the groups I hunt in.
Why it matters
Rotation turns "the market is fine" into "look here".
It also carries one distinction that is worth real money. A group can reach the top of the table in two very different ways, and only one of them is leadership.
A group bouncing off its lows still has sellers waiting above, people who bought higher and want out. A group at new highs has none of them.
The three numbers I read
—
Rank — how the group is performing against its own recent history, with a faster reading for the current week on top.
—
Leading — strong, and within a few percent of its highs. This is breakout leadership.
—
Off the floor — the same strong numbers, but still fifteen or twenty-five percent below its highs. That is a recovery bounce dressed up as leadership. The table marks the difference. Respect the mark.
How I use it
—
Hunt only where the group ranks high and sits near its highs. Two or three groups, not ten.
—
Inside the group, buy the leader — the highest-ranked name. Not the one that "hasn't moved yet". It has not moved for a reason.
—
Watch the weakest name in the group anyway. When it breaks down first, the leader's clock has started.
Common mistakes
Reading the rank and skipping the distance-to-highs beside it. That is how a bounce gets bought at full leadership size.
Hunting in ten groups at once. That is the same as hunting in none.
Buying the cheapest name in a great group. The ranking already told you which name the money chose.
Takeaway
The rotation table is the map between the market and the stock. Hunt where it is green and near highs. Treat a bounce off the lows as a different, lesser trade.

Relative strength
Buy what is already outperforming, not what looks cheap
What this is
Relative strength compares a stock against the market over a set period, then ranks it against every other stock.
It does not tell me how far a stock has travelled on its own. It tells me where it sits in the field.
Why it matters
Big buyers leave tracks long before the news explains why. This is where those tracks show up.
A stock that refuses to make new lows while the market is making them is being bought by someone.
The ranking is published every day, it is free, and it moves before the story does.
How I read it
—
The floor for a candidate — top 20% of the market at minimum, top 10% preferred. No exceptions for a good story.
—
Three periods, not one — one month, three months, six months. One hot week is noise. Three periods agreeing is a footprint.
—
The line against the market — how a stock behaves on the market's worst days beats any number.
Compare every number to the market
A stock's gain means nothing on its own. You have to know what the market did over the same period.
Say a stock is up 10% this month. On its own that sounds ordinary. Now look at the market: it fell 6% in that same month. So the stock beat the market by 16%. Nothing is lifting it. People are buying that stock while they sell everything else.
So I check the market first, before I pick any number:
The market went up a lot
Most stocks will show a big gain. Set your minimum higher, or your filter lets almost everything through.
The market was flat
The stock's gain is roughly how much it beat the market by. Read it as it is.
The market went down
Any stock that is up has already beaten the market. This is when the filter helps you most, and when the fewest stocks pass it.
Picking your minimum
There is no fixed number. I set a minimum gain, then raise or lower it until the list is short enough to chart tonight. I use the same three periods as the ranking: one month, three months, six months.
Here is one screen, run twice. The market was down 6% both times. The only thing I changed is the minimum 1-month gain:
—
Minimum 20% — these stocks beat the market by 26%. Twenty-five stocks pass. That is an evening's work.
—
Minimum 30% — these beat the market by 36%. Eight stocks pass. That is a focus list.
Use a bigger number for a longer period. Beating the market by 20% in one month is hard. Beating it by 20% over six months is not. Same idea, higher bar.
How I use it
The ranking is my first filter. A stock that fails it never gets charted.
When two stocks both pass, I look at how they behaved on the market's worst days. A stock that made a higher low while the market made a lower one is showing me the next leader. That is worth more to me than a few points of rank.
Common mistakes
Judging a stock's gain on its own. Up 5% sounds good. But if the market went up 8% over the same period, that stock is behind. It is weak, not strong. Always compare it to the market.
Buying a falling stock because it looks cheap. It is cheap because people are selling it. Cheap is not the same as ready.
Takeaway
The market ranks every stock every day. Read the ranking. Do not argue with it.

The funnel
Three scans, one watchlist, a short focus list
The point of scanning
There are thousands of stocks listed in the US. On any given day, fewer than fifty are worth my attention.
A scan does not find me a trade. It takes the whole market down to a watchlist I can actually read.
How I think about it
I do not scan for stocks I hope will move. I scan for stocks already moving.
Strength leads to more strength. Weakness leads to more weakness.
So I look for the stocks doing what every big winner did before it ran: outperforming the market, building a base, and coming out of a quiet stretch on heavy volume.
The three scans I run
I split the work into three buckets. Same three, every evening.
—
(1) The movers ladder — top gainers over one, three and six months. This finds what has already moved.
—
(2) The growth scan — sales and earnings growing hard, high relative strength, price already near its 52-week high. This finds why a stock should keep moving.
—
(3) The pre-market gappers — run around 9:25 New York time, when the real list has formed. This is the scan built for surprise catalysts.
Why I run them at night
I scan in the evening, when the market is closed and nothing is moving.
Run daily, the same leaders walk past my screen again and again. So my memory does the compounding. By the time one of them triggers, it is not a new idea I have to judge under pressure. It is a stock I already know.
The scan builds the watchlist. Only the chart builds the trade.
Scan 1 — the movers ladder, filter by filter
This is one saved screen, sorted three different ways. Six filters do all the work. Everything else stays on default.
Price over $3
Cuts the sub-$3 tier. Thin books, wide spreads, and the games that live down there. I hunt where institutions can actually buy.
Market cap over $300M
The smallest size a fund can buy. Below this there are no fund flows to follow.
ADR over 4%
ADR is how much a stock moves in a normal day. It has to move enough to pay for its stop within days. A stock that travels 1% a day cannot.
Average volume over 500K shares
Enough shares change hands that I can get in and out without moving the price.
Price × average volume over $5M
The dollar version of the same check. I use both, because each catches what the other misses. Share volume alone lets in cheap lottery tickets. Dollar volume alone lets in thinly traded expensive ones.
Sort by performance
The ladder itself. Same stocks, three views. Sort by 1 month, then 3 months, then 6 months.
Reading the three sorts together
The overlap between the sorts is the real signal.
—
Near the top of all three — a leader that keeps leading.
—
High on 3 and 6 months, cooled on 1 month — a winner resting. These are tomorrow's entries.
Tightening the ladder
887 stocks is a starting pool, not a watchlist. Every filter is a lever. I raise them until each sort holds under about fifty.
—
Price $3 to $10 — removes the junk tier. The leaders I want almost never live under ten dollars.
—
Market cap $300M to $1B — institutional names only.
—
ADR 4% to 5-6% — keeps only the stocks that can pay fast.
—
Dollar volume $5M to $20M or more — my own bar for trading at real size.
—
Add a minimum gain that matches the sort — 1 month at 30% or more, 3 months at 50%, 6 months at 100%. Now the list holds stocks that already proved it, not everything merely ranked.
—
Optional: price above a rising 10- and 20-day line — keeps only what is still going up. This one cuts hardest.
Scan 2 — the growth scan
You will hear this one called a CANSLIM scan. If that word is new, here is all it means.
CANSLIM is a stock-picking checklist that came out of a study of the biggest winning stocks in market history. The researchers asked one question: before these stocks ran, what did they all have in common? Seven things kept showing up, and each letter stands for one of them — growing earnings this quarter, growing earnings each year, something new happening at the company, a small number of shares available, being the leader rather than the laggard, funds buying it, and the overall market going up.
You do not have to memorise the letters. Notice instead that you already know most of them from this book. Leader not laggard is piece 04. Market going up is piece 01. Funds buying is what the group lists show you.
So my growth scan is just those ideas turned into filters. The movers ladder finds what has moved. This one finds why it should keep moving.
Eight conditions, all required at once. A stock passes every line or it is not on the list.
Price over $3
The same junk-tier cut the ladder makes.
Market cap over $300M
Funds cannot build a position below this, so their buying never shows up in the price.
Sales growth over 25% versus a year ago
The engine. Sales, not profit — profit can be manufactured by cost-cutting for a few quarters, sales cannot. 25% is where a quarter is genuinely different from the year before.
Average volume over 500K shares (60 days)
The liquidity floor, measured over a longer window than the ladder so one news spike cannot sneak a thin stock through.
Price above the 50-day line
The stock has to already be going up. This one filter is what stops the screen filling with cheap growth stories still in downtrends.
Volatility over 3%
It has to move enough to pay for its stop, however good the numbers read.
Float under 150M shares
Float is how many shares are actually available to trade. The same buying against a smaller float produces a far bigger move. This is the filter that turns good growth into violent price action.
70% or more above its 52-week low
Proof the market has already re-rated it. Leaders break out from strength, not from repair.
Tightening the growth scan
Same rule as before. If a scan returns more stocks than I can chart in an evening, it is too loose. I raise the bars until it holds under about fifty.
—
Sales growth 25% to 40% or more — every step up cuts the survivor count hard, which is the whole job.
—
Float 150M down to 50M or less — the strongest lever on this screen. Scarcity is what makes the moves violent.
—
Volatility 3% to 5% or more — keeps only the stocks that can pay within days.
—
Add price above the 200-day line — the long-term trend agreeing with the 50-day.
—
Lift the volume floor well past 500K — so every survivor can be traded at real size.

The list I actually watch
All of this scanning ends in one place: a watchlist that stays open on my screen all day.
Mine is shared, so you can see what this routine actually produces. It is the same list I trade from, and it changes as stocks earn their place or lose it.
Open the VIV focus list on TradingView
It is split into two groups, not ranked as one long list:
—
Strong stocks (stalking) — already leading. I am waiting for a trigger to buy them.
—
Mediocre stocks (setting up) — not strong enough to buy yet, but tightening up. I keep them in front of me so I notice the day they change.
Two groups instead of one, because a list that mixes "buy this today" with "maybe next month" makes you hesitate at the wrong moment.
I keep the focus list to six to twelve stocks. If everything is interesting, nothing is. A stock that passes a scan but fails the chart stays on the watchlist. One that clears both moves to the focus list and gets a written trigger and stop.
Common mistakes
Scanning during the session and calling it research. By then the day's noise is picking your list.
Letting the focus list grow past a dozen because nothing wants to be cut.
Skipping the evening after a losing day. That is exactly when the routine is doing the most work.
Takeaway
Three scans run every night beat forty scans you never run. What the routine really produces is not trades — it is knowing the leaders before they move.

The earnings staircase
Growing sales and profits — the fuel underneath the chart
What this is
The staircase is a company's recent quarters laid side by side, oldest to newest.
What I want to see is sales growth speeding up — each stair higher than the last — with profit following sales rather than replacing it.
This is the fuel underneath a chart. It is either there or it is not.
Why it matters
The chart triggers every trade in this book. The staircase does a different job.
It tells me whether a stock is worth holding for weeks and adding to, or whether it is a quick trade I should cut quickly.
Growth decides how long I hold and how much I add. It never decides when I buy.
What counts as fuel
—
Speeding up, not just big — 20% then 60% then 110% beats a flat 50% every quarter.
—
Sales leading profit — profit growing without sales growing is cost-cutting, and cost-cutting runs out.
—
A one-sentence story the market can repeat, attached to a group that is being funded.
—
Surprise — the violent moves come where nobody expected anything. Strong numbers from a stock everyone had forgotten about.
How I use it
The chart still decides when I buy. The growth decides how much I am willing to sit through.
Great chart, no growth behind it: I take the quick trade and cut it quickly.
Great chart, growing every quarter, in a group being bought: that is the one I work.
Common mistakes
Quoting a staircase from a headline or from memory. Build the table yourself.
Reading one enormous quarter as acceleration when the quarters around it are flat.
Buying the fundamentals before the chart gives a trigger. Fuel is not a signal. It is a reason to be patient once a signal arrives.
Takeaway
The chart decides when you buy. The growth decides how much you expect from it. Check the actual numbers — never trade growth you have only read about.

The no-buy list
The trades we refuse to take
What this is
The no-buy list is a fixed set of deal-breakers, written down before I need them.
It is not a score to weigh up. One "yes" ends the conversation, no matter how good the chart looks or how loud the story is.
Why it matters
The trades you refuse are half your edge.
Every line on this list was paid for by a loss that felt obvious afterwards. The list exists so I cannot buy the same loss twice.
I do not buy when…
—
The market check is red. Breakouts are failing more often than they work.
—
The stock is below its 200-day line. That line separates a stock in an uptrend from one still repairing damage. I stay on the right side of it.
—
The group is on neither list, or the rotation table flags it as a bounce off the lows.
—
The stock is stretched — four or more daily ranges above its 50-day line, or more than 5% past the proper buy point. Fresh, not stretched.
—
The base is late in the run — a third or fourth base. The same shape means far less than it did the first time.
—
The base is wide and loose — deep pullbacks, overlapping bars, no tightening.
—
The breakout has no volume behind it.
—
Earnings land within five sessions. The exception is when the trade is the earnings reaction, planned as one.
—
My total open risk is already at its limit. There is no room for another stop.
—
The honest reason is boredom, a losing day, or wanting to feel like I am doing something. The market owes me nothing.
How I run it
The list sits above my desk. I run it before the order exists, not after the fill.
Ten questions take seconds. I answer them in order, out loud.
One yes means no. Nine clean answers do not outvote one bad one.
Common mistakes
Overriding one line because the other nine are clean.
Only checking the list on trades that already feel shaky.
Rewriting a line mid-session so the trade in front of you passes. The list gets edited in the journal, on a quiet evening, with evidence.
Takeaway
The mistake is never the rule. It is the click that ignored it. If you cannot say why you are buying in one sentence, you are not buying.

House
The volatility contraction pattern (VCP)
After a correction or pullback — contractions tightening into the pivot
What this is
VCP stands for volatility contraction pattern. That sounds technical. It is not.
It just means a stock's swings are getting smaller. It runs up, pulls back hard, runs up, pulls back a bit less, then a bit less again. The pullbacks shrink each time until the stock is barely moving.
Then it breaks out. That break is the trade.
I trade every setup in this book. But in my opinion this one is among the easiest to master, so it is a good place to start. You will see it all through my model book.
Why it works
Every pullback removes another group of sellers.
The first one scares out the nervous holders. The second one shakes out the people who bought that first dip. By the last one, almost nobody left wants to sell.
So the shrinking pullbacks are sellers running out, happening in plain sight. That is why I can read this pattern before it breaks.
The tightening is the edge. The breakout is only the confirmation.
Stage 1 — the move before the base
—
A real run of 30% or more into the base.
—
That run should be clean and steady, not a zig-zag.
—
A young trend — the first, second or third base of the run. Not a late one.
Stage 2 — the shrinking pullbacks
—
Each pullback clearly shallower than the one before. Roughly halving as you go left to right: 25%, then 12%, then 6%.
—
The last pullback is tight — 10% deep or less. More of a drift than a swing.
—
Volume drying up through the base, with the quietest volume of all inside that final pullback.
—
Higher lows into the buy point, price riding its rising 10-, 20- and 50-day lines.
—
Nice extras: inside bars right before the trigger, and the 10-, 20- and 50-day lines coming together at the buy point.
Stage 3 — the trigger
I draw a flat line across the highs of that final pullback. That is my buy point.
Then I want a day that expands 4% or more — a bar visibly bigger than the last five to ten — closing in the top 30% of its range, on volume above the day before.
Two more conditions. No more than two up-days into it, so I am not buying on day three. And a catalyst within a couple of sessions is a bonus, never a requirement.
How I trade it
The nightly scans find the candidates. The three stages qualify them. The entry checklist times the buy: the opening-range trigger on the breakout day, stop at that day's low.
Until the expansion day arrives, this is a watchlist name and nothing more.
Size is worked out from the stop, exactly as in piece 14. What the age of the base changes is whether I take the trade at all — a fourth base is on my no-buy list.
Common mistakes
Calling one quiet week a VCP. It has to be a sequence. Without pullbacks that get smaller, it is just a range.
Buying loose bases because the story is tight.
Jumping in before the expansion day exists.
Treating a late base like a first one. The older the trend, the shorter its fuse.
Takeaway
The tightening is the edge; the breakout is only the confirmation. Buy the break of a real sequence of shrinking pullbacks — not the hope of one.

The high tight flag (HTF)
Continuation momentum — the pause inside a powerful advance
What this is
A high tight flag has two parts and nothing else.
The pole — a violent run. Roughly a double, 90 to 100% or more, inside about eight weeks.
The flag — a shallow, tight, quiet drift near those highs while the stock does almost nothing.
The trade is the break out of that flag. Like the VCP, I find this one easier to master than most — which is why I point newer traders at these two first.
One thing to be clear about: this is a continuation setup. The trend is already running and the flag is the pause inside it. It is not a bottom and it is not a turn.
Why it works
A stock doubles in weeks. Then it refuses to give any of it back.
Think about what that means. Not one holder is willing to sell into a 100% gain. Nobody is taking the money.
No other pattern says that as loudly. The whole edge is in that refusal to sell. So the tightness of the pause is not a detail — it is the setup.
What I need to see
—
The pole — roughly a double, 90 to 100% or more, in eight weeks or less. Orderly, strong closes, bars that do not overlap much. One vertical spike day is not a pole.
—
The flag is shallow — no more than 20 to 25% off the pole high. Deeper than that and it is a correction, not a flag.
—
The flag is brief — three to five weeks, and not stretching much past eight. Time is the enemy here. The longer it drifts, the less the pole means.
—
The flag is tight — a clean drift. No jagged bars, volume fading through it, narrow days into the buy point. It must not round out and it must not cut deep.
—
The trigger — a break above the pattern high, not an early poke through the flag line, on rising volume.
—
It is the first or second flag of the run, not a late one.
There is no partial credit here. The moment the flag rounds out or cuts deep, the setup is gone.
How I trade it
Because it is a continuation setup, the trend that built the pole has to still be intact when the flag breaks.
The entry is timed like every other setup: opening-range trigger, volume confirming, stop at the low of the day.
One thing to watch. The run into it was so steep that price often stretches far above its 50-day line within days. So the profit-taking rules in Part 5 come into play early.
Common mistakes
Forcing the label onto a stock that ran 40% and paused. The pole is a measured number. The label means nothing until you have measured it.
Tolerating a flag that widens, rounds out, or cuts deep and calling it "still constructive".
Buying the flag line instead of the pattern high.
Treating a late flag like a first one. The same shape at the end of a run means nothing like the same thing.
Takeaway
The pole is the claim; the tight flag is the proof nobody wants to sell it. Deep, loose or rounded is not a flag — it is a top forming.

The undercut and rally (UnR)
Buying a leader on a pullback, after it shakes people out
What this is
This is my second way into a leader I already missed.
The stock pulls back to its rising short-term moving averages. It dips below a previous low or an obvious support level. Then it closes back above it.
The entry is not the pullback. It is not the dip below either. The entry is the moment price takes that level back.
Why it works
The dip below does something no buyer can do on his own. It triggers all the stop orders sitting under that obvious level, and it scares out the nervous holders.
So when price recovers, there is far less selling left in the way.
It also gives me a tight stop. The low that was just made is the exact level the trade is arguing about. That is a few percent of risk on a leader in the middle of its trend.
What I need to see
—
Moving averages still rising — the short ones sloping up, price still above the longer ones. A pullback into flat or falling averages is a different, worse trade.
—
A young trend — the first, second or third pullback. By the fourth, the easy part of the trend is behind us.
—
Volume drying up on the way down — a heavy-volume pullback is selling, not resting, and the level will not hold.
—
A real dip below, then a real reclaim — price has to break the prior low and close back above it, ideally on a reversal bar. No dip means no shakeout. No reclaim means no trade.
—
Stop at the pullback low, under one daily range. If the chart demands a wider stop, the chart is telling me no.
—
Everything else still intact — group still being bought, relative strength still holding, trend unbroken.
How I trade it
I let it dip below. I let the weak hands get stopped out. Then I buy the move back through the level.
I buy nothing while price is still falling. The reclaim is the entire signal. If it never comes, there was never a trade.
One warning. Because the stop is so tight, the maths gives me a big share count for my usual risk. That is exactly why I must not force this setup onto a stock that has not earned it.
Common mistakes
Buying while price is still falling. That turns a tight stop into a very wide one.
Mistaking a weak bounce in a broken stock for a pullback. A pullback keeps the uptrend intact. A breakdown has already lost it.
Taking the fourth or fifth pullback of an old trend as though it were the first.
Takeaway
The dip below shakes people out; taking the level back is the proof. Buy the proof, not the hope.

The episodic pivot (EP)
The rare one — surprise catalyst on a neglected name
What this is
An episodic pivot is a buyable gap up. In plain terms: the market suddenly has to reprice a stock almost nobody owned.
Genuinely new information lands on a forgotten stock. It opens 10% or more above yesterday's close. Volume floods in so fast the stock trades a normal day's worth inside the first thirty minutes.
This is the setup my pre-market gapper scan is built for, and it is why that scan runs before every open.
Why it works
These are the rarest setups in this book. Only a handful a year.
The gap itself is not the move. It is the announcement that the repricing has started, and that usually takes weeks to play out.
The good news is that everything which makes these work can be checked on the day.
What I need to see
—
Neglect before it — flat or basing for months with no big run behind it. A gap on a stock everybody already loves is not a surprise, it is extension risk. And it should come out of an orderly base, not out of a falling knife.
—
The first surprise — no recent gap of this kind on the chart. The first one reprices the stock. The third one is already priced in.
—
The gap itself — 10% or more visible, or a 4%-plus expansion on an earnings day.
—
Volume that proves it — a huge bar that dwarfs the recent ones, at least three times normal by the close, with pre-market volume leading the way.
—
It holds the gap — closes in the upper part of the day's range, above the prior day. Closing back inside the gap is a rejection.
—
A stop that fits — a tight level available within about one to one-and-a-half daily ranges, and clear air overhead with no resistance to chew through.
Two ways in
—
Day one — the five-minute opening-range high, stop at the day's low.
—
The delayed entry — when the gap is too big to size sanely against the day's low, I wait. I let the first constructive day form, the first tight pause or higher low above the gap, and I use that instead.
Missing the first hour costs very little here. The real move takes weeks.
Common mistakes
Buying the open before any range exists, then finding the stop is a third of the position.
Calling a rehashed headline new information.
Taking a gap on a stock that already ran, where the news confirms rather than surprises.
Holding one that closed back inside the gap, hoping tomorrow fixes it. If it hesitates, it lied.
Takeaway
Surprise is the edge and neglect is the proof of it. If it hesitates, it lied — the real ones leave immediately.

The parabolic short and the breakdown (PS)
The short side, taken only after the top is in
What this is
This is the one setup I take from the short side. Shorting means betting a stock goes down, so I make money when it falls.
It comes in two versions.
—
The parabolic — a stock has gone vertical, far above every rising moving average, and finally cracks.
—
The breakdown — a former leader has already topped, been sold into, rolled over, and then loses a level it had been leaning on.
Both are the same trade in different clothing. I am selling something that has already broken.
Why it works — and the warning first
Let me say this plainly. This is not shorting strength.
Shorting a leader because it "looks too high" is how accounts end. A stock can stay expensive far longer than your position can survive.
The whole idea here is that the top has already happened. The heavy selling has shown up. The moving averages have turned down. The support level has broken. I am trading what comes after.
Everything below exists to prove the top happened before I take the trade.
Version 1 — the parabolic
—
A vertical run: 50 to 100% on a big stock, 300% or more on a small one, in days to weeks.
—
Three to five or more up days in a row into the top.
—
Visibly far above all of its rising moving averages. Obvious on sight, not a matter of opinion.
—
Trigger — the first crack. A big red bar, or a break of the opening range in my direction. Intraday, a failure at the volume-weighted average price confirms it.
—
Structure — stop at the day's extreme, one daily range or less, with enough room down to the 10- or 20-day line for the target to pay.
Version 2 — the failed leader
—
Selling at the top: failed highs and big red high-volume days.
—
The averages roll over — price below the 10-, 20- and 50-day lines, with those lines turning down.
—
Lower highs forming into the support level, and the 50-day lost with no recovery within a few sessions.
—
The stock goes down on days the market goes up. That sign usually comes before the break.
—
The level wears out — three or more tests of the same support, with weak low-volume bounces in between that cannot get back to the falling 20-day line.
—
Trigger — it breaks support and closes below it. Not an intraday poke. On a bigger down bar, volume above the prior day, closing in the bottom 30% of the range. A gap down through the level counts.
You do not have to trade this one
Everything else in this book is a long. This is the only short, and it is optional.
In my experience shorts win less often than longs. So if you never take a single one, you have not missed the system. Learn the long setups first and come back to this when they are second nature.
How I size it
The short side is smaller by default. Half the per-trade risk of a long, and my total short exposure stays under half of what I would carry long in a healthy market.
Targets are the 10- and 20-day lines. I am not waiting for a collapse.
These trades work in exactly the markets where my long positions should already be shrinking. That is why the setup exists at all.
Common mistakes
Shorting a strong stock because it is up a lot. Up a lot is not a signal.
Selling the intraday dip below support before a close below it, then getting run over when it recovers.
Taking the first bounce off support as the breakdown.
Shorting at the same size you would go long. The risk is not the same — a short can keep going against you without limit.
Takeaway
I do not short strength — I sell a chart that has already broken. No top, no trade.

When to walk in
How we enter
Trigger, volume, stop — checked in that order, every time
What this is
An entry is not a price. It is the moment buyers show they are in control.
Three things have to agree before that moment counts: a trigger, a confirmation, and a stop.
The three are the same on every setup in this book.
Why it matters
The entry is the one moment where the risk is completely in your hands.
A trigger on its own proves very little. Price pokes through levels all day long.
What separates a real breakout from a poke is how many people showed up. That is why the volume check is not optional. Without it, the prettiest trigger is a trap.
The three checks
—
Trigger — the five-minute opening-range high. The first five-minute bar of the day sets a range. The break of its high is the earliest proof of real demand. On the biggest gaps I use the one-minute range instead.
—
Confirmation — relative volume, measured honestly. Today's volume against the same time of day on a normal day, not against a full day's average. What I want: roughly 40% of a full session's volume already done inside the first thirty minutes.
—
Stop — the low of the day, placed with the order. The day's low is where the day's idea died. If that distance is wider than about one daily range, the trade does not fit. Pass, or take fewer shares.
How I run it
I check all three in order, out loud, before I click. One sentence each.
All three, or none. A trigger without volume is a pass. A trigger with volume but a stop wider than one daily range is either a smaller position or no position.
Notice that I know the stop before I know the share count. That is deliberate. The next piece turns that distance into a number of shares.
Common mistakes
Buying mid-bar with no trigger.
Entering at lunch on the morning's news.
Deciding the size before you know the stop. That is backwards, as the next piece shows.
Takeaway
Trigger, volume, stop — one sentence each, before the click. If any of the three is missing, so is the trade.

When to walk in
Sizing is arithmetic
Decide what you can lose first; the share count follows
What this is
Position sizing answers one question: how much do I lose if I am wrong?
I decide that number before the trade exists. Then the share count works itself out.
Size comes out of the risk. Not out of how much I like the trade.
The formula
Shares = (Account × Risk%) ÷ (Entry − Stop)
A $100,000 account risking 0.3% is $300 of risk. Entry $50, stop $48. That is $2 per share. $300 ÷ $2 = 150 shares.
I do it out loud, every time, before the order exists.
My anchors
—
Per-trade risk: a fraction of one percent. I anchor at a quarter to a third of a percent. Tighter after losses. Never above one percent for any reason.
—
Wilder stocks size themselves down automatically. A wilder stock has a wider stop, and a wider stop means fewer shares. Same risk, different share counts. That is the system working, not a mistake.
—
Position size is an output, never a target. "How many shares can I afford" is the question that ends accounts.
The other half nobody thinks about
Your risk percentage decides how much money you can lose. It does not decide how big your position is.
That comes from the second number in the formula: the distance from your entry to your stop. The two work together.
Here is why it matters. Same $100,000 account. Same 0.3% risk, so $300 on the line every time. Same $50 stock. The only thing that changes is how far away the stop sits:
Stop $1.50 away (3%)
I can buy 200 shares. If the stock goes up 25%, I make $2,500. That is 8 times what I risked. If it goes up 50%, 17 times.
Stop $1.00 away (2%)
I can buy 300 shares. The same 25% move now makes $3,750 — 12 times what I risked. At 50%, 25 times.
Stop $0.75 away (1.5%)
I can buy 400 shares. The same 25% move makes $5,000 — 17 times what I risked. At 50%, 33 times.
Read that again. I risked exactly $300 every time. The stock made exactly the same move every time.
But the closer my stop was, the more I made. Half the distance, double the profit.
So how do you get a closer stop?
Not by picking a smaller number. The stop goes where the trade is proven wrong — under the low of the day. You cannot move it closer just because you want more shares.
You get a closer stop by buying closer to it.
Buy the moment the trigger happens, while the low of the day is still right below you. Wait an hour and the stock has run — now the low of the day is twice as far away. Same trade, half the shares, half the payoff.
That is exactly why the entry check exists: only take the trade if the low of the day is within about one normal day's range. A good setup taken late is a worse trade than the same setup taken on time.
One warning
A closer stop is also easier to hit. Stocks wobble.
If you squeeze the stop just to buy more shares, you will get knocked out of trades that were right.
The stop has to be both close and in the right place — under a real level on the chart, not at a number you chose because you liked the share count.
How I run it
The order never changes: setup, then stop, then arithmetic, then order.
If the maths gives me a share count that feels too small to bother with, the real answer is that the stop is too wide for my account. Not that the risk percentage should go up.
That percentage moves for one reason only: a drawdown.
Common mistakes
The same share count on every trade regardless of stop distance.
Sizing up to win it back.
Confusing a big position with a big idea.
Takeaway
Two numbers set your size: what you risk, and how far the stop is. Fix the loss first, buy close to the stop, then work out the shares.

When to walk in
The three limits
One trade, all trades together, and total money in the market
What this is
Three different ceilings protect the account. They are not the same number, and people mix them up constantly.
—
Risk — what one stop-out costs you.
—
Total open risk — what all your open positions cost you if they all stopped out today.
—
Exposure — how much money you have in the market at all.
Three questions, three answers, three separate limits.
Why you need all three
Each one protects against a different disaster.
The first stops one trade from doing real damage. The second stops a single bad day, where everything falls at once, from doing it. The third stops the slow bleed of being too heavily invested in a weak market.
Any one of them can block a trade the other two would have allowed.
Limit one — risk on a single trade
The share of the account one stop-out is allowed to cost. The sizing formula in the last piece takes care of it.
Limit two — total open risk
Add up every open position's distance to its stop. That total is what today costs you if everything stops out at once.
I cap it at roughly 2% of the account.
One catch: stocks that move together count as one. Three positions in the same group is really one trade held in three names.
Limit three — total exposure
How much of the account is invested overnight at all. My standing ceiling is roughly 30% of the account.
This counts every overnight position, including the ones whose stops are already at break-even. A stock can gap open below your stop and skip straight past it. Risk-free is not exposure-free.
Inside that ceiling, the number moves with the market check. Full when the market is strong. A fraction when it is mixed. Near zero when it is weak.
How I run it
I answer all three before I enter, in that order.
If a new setup would push me over the open-risk cap, it waits. Or I tighten a stop on something I already hold to make room.
As stops move up, open risk falls by itself and the room comes back without me doing anything.
Common mistakes
Treating the three as one number. This is how a trader who feels disciplined takes an undisciplined loss. A comfortable-looking amount of money invested can still be carrying three times the intended risk.
Waving through a fourth position in the same group and calling it diversification.
Going over a limit because the setup looks excellent. The limits exist for exactly the trades that feel worth breaking them for.
Takeaway
Risk on one trade, risk across all of them, and how much you hold overnight. Three questions before every entry — three chances for the rules to save you.

When to walk in
Cutting losers
Small when wrong, instantly
What this is
The stop goes where the trade is proven wrong.
That is the day's low that defined the entry, or the pullback low, or the level that undoes the whole idea if it breaks. Never where you simply stop feeling comfortable.
It is a price on the chart, decided before you own anything. It is the only part of a trade you fully control.
Why it matters more than anything else here
You can be average at everything else in this book and survive if you get this piece right. Get this one wrong and being excellent everywhere else will not save you.
The maths of losses is lopsided:
—
Down 10% needs 11% to get back.
—
Down 25% needs 33%.
—
Down 50% needs a double.
Small losses are the cost of doing business. Big ones take months to undo.
My rules
—
The stop goes in with the entry order. A trade without a stop sitting in the market is not a position — it is a guess you are paying for.
—
Width ceiling: about one daily range. Wider means the trade does not fit. Fewer shares, or pass.
—
The stop never moves down. Not once, not for a close call, not for a story.
—
Averaging down is not a strategy. It is the same mistake at better prices.
The signal before the stop
There is a second signal worth acting on before the stop ever triggers.
The trades that work tend to work straight away.
A new position that just sits there going nowhere is telling you something. Cutting it back early is a decision, not a panic.
Common mistakes
Widening the stop to give the trade room. That is the trade telling you it does not fit. The answer is fewer shares, not more rope.
Cancelling the resting order to "watch it manually".
Re-entering minutes after the stop with no new trigger.
Treating a stop-out as a judgement on you. It is just what it costs to find out.
Takeaway
Decide what wrong looks like before you enter, then honour it without arguing. The skill is not being right — it is being small when you are wrong.

Piece 17 of 21
After you move in
Trim into strength
Taking profit while the stock is still going up
What this is
Trimming means selling part of a winner while it is still going up.
The point is that the rest can keep running without your whole position depending on one pullback.
Two things tell me when: the first few days after a breakout, and how far price has stretched above its 50-day line.
Why it matters
When a breakout works, it usually pays fast. A sharp move over the first three to five days.
After that, the odds of a pullback or a stall go up.
The traders who never take anything off are the ones who watch their best entries give it all back.
Trigger one — the first few days
When the trade is up 3 to 5 times what I risked, I sell 30 to 50% of it. If that has not happened by day three to five, I do it anyway on the clock.
Then I move the stop on what is left to break-even.
Now the trade cannot lose. That changes how you hold it more than any technique will.
The profit trigger leads. The clock is only the backstop, never the other way round.
Trigger two — how stretched it is
The second trigger needs one indicator, and it is the most useful number on my chart for taking profit.
It measures how far price sits above its 50-day line, but not in plain percent. Plain percent lies to you.
Think about two stocks, both sitting 10% above their 50-day line. The first one normally moves 1% a day. The second normally moves 7% a day. The first is enormously stretched. The second has barely gone anywhere. Same 10%, completely different situations.
So instead of the raw percent, I divide it by how much that stock moves on a normal day. That gives me a multiple, and it puts every stock on one scale.
Distance above the 50-day line ÷ the stock's normal daily range = the multiple.
A reading of 5 means price is five normal days' worth of movement above its 50-day line. That means the same thing on a quiet stock as on a wild one, which is exactly why it works.
The bands I use
Under 4 — the launchpad
Price is close to its 50-day line. This is where bases form and where I am allowed to buy. Fresh, not stretched.
Around 5 — stretched
No adds here, and I start tightening. The easy part is behind it.
7.5 to 8 — rare air
Genuinely unusual. This is where my trims start scaling up.
Around 10 on a stock
Sell aggressively into the move.
6 to 7 on an index or leveraged fund
The same "extreme" level, but it arrives sooner. Indexes and leveraged funds are made of many stocks, so they simply do not stretch as far as a single one. Do not wait for 10 on these — it is not coming.
One thing that surprises people
A high reading is not a sell signal. It is a trim signal. There is a difference.
Stretched stocks tend to go quiet for about a week and then outperform for months afterwards. So the stretch is a sign of quality, not a sign of a top.
That is why I trim into it and never short it. And it is why I never chase it either — buying at 8 means my stop is eight normal days away, which is the opposite of piece 14.
My own record says the same thing. Across 200 of my trades, exits taken at 7 times or deeper won 76% of the time and averaged +10.7%. Exits taken between 2 and 5 won 51% and averaged +2.3%.
The judgement, in one question
Is this a healthy trend or a stretched one?
A healthy trend grinds upward with normal rests along its moving averages. Hold it.
A stretched one goes near-vertical, closes right at the highs, and volume keeps building. Take some off.
That difference is measurable. So measure it rather than guessing.
Common mistakes
Selling on a feeling a day or two early, then watching the best part of the move happen without you.
Taking the trim and forgetting the other half. Moving the stop to break-even is what makes the trim worth taking.
Holding the whole position through a vertical run because it "still looks strong". That is exactly what the multiple is there to catch.
Takeaway
Sell some while everyone is excited, and hold the rest on the rules. Selling it all gives up the big winner; holding it all gives back the gain. The answer is a partial.

Trail the rest
One moving average, daily closes only
What this is
The runner is the part of the position I keep after taking profit.
I manage it with one trailing moving average, matched to how fast the stock moves, read on daily closes only.
That line is not a target. It is simply the level that, once lost, ends the trade.
Why it matters
The runner is where a good year separates from an average one.
You cannot know in advance which winner keeps going for months. So every runner gets its chance to prove it — judged by a line on the chart, not by an opinion.
How I do it
—
The lines — the 9- and 21-day exponential moving averages for the trail itself, read against the 10-, 20- and 50-day simple averages that show the trend. Fast movers follow the faster line. Steadier ones follow the slower.
—
Pick one line and stick with it. Choose it when you take profit. Swapping to a slower line later is just an excuse to keep holding.
—
Daily closes only. Quick dips through the line during the day are how the market shakes out impatient holders. Only the close counts.
—
The trade ends when the closes say so — the first clear daily close below the line, or the pattern of higher lows breaking.
How I run it
A quick dip below the line that closes back above it is not an exit. Allowing that is what keeps me in the trends worth holding.
I check the line once, after the close, and decide the next day then.
Nothing about a runner is decided in the middle of the day.
Common mistakes
Using the fastest line on a slow stock, and getting shaken out of a good trend by normal noise.
Tightening the stop because you are bored.
Selling a runner because it is "up a lot". Being up a lot is the whole point.
Takeaway
One line, daily closes, and forgive the quick dips. Give your winners every chance to keep going.

Adds and pyramids
Adding to a winner, only when it proves itself again
What this is
An add is a second or third buy into a position that is already working. A pyramid is a series of adds, each one smaller than the last.
Either way you end up with one position, at one average price, with one risk number. Never a pile of separate trades in the same stock.
Why it matters
Adding is how a good position becomes a great one. It is also how a careless one dies.
The difference is entirely in when you do it.
An add made when the chart gives a fresh reason increases the position without increasing the risk. An add made on a feeling increases both.
The sizing
Each add is smaller than the one before: a full first buy, then half to three-quarters of it, then a quarter to half.
That keeps my average price near the original entry.
Every add also forces the stop on the whole position higher, so the total risk never grows. If the stop cannot move up enough to keep the maths working, I am not allowed the add. How strongly I feel about it does not enter the calculation.
Where the adds happen
—
The first tight pause against the short moving averages after the initial move. The break out of that pause is the trigger.
—
The next base — a stair-step pause and a fresh expansion. Each new coil is a new contract.
The rule fits in one sentence: add only when the stock proves itself again, at a fresh point on the chart, with the whole position's risk still inside the three limits.
Common mistakes
Adding into near-vertical days far above the moving averages. That is the zone where piece 17 says sell, not buy. You add when a stock pauses. You sell when it stretches. Getting those two backwards is the whole difference between building a position and blowing one up.
Adding without moving the stop, which quietly turns one disciplined position into three undisciplined ones.
Takeaway
The stock earns the add; you do not decide it on a feeling. The chart triggers it and the risk maths approves it.

Keeping the roof on
Cutting size in a losing streak
Decided in advance, so it never has to be argued about
What this is
This is my written-in-advance response to a run of losses: a schedule that cuts trade size in steps as the drawdown gets deeper.
Because it is decided beforehand, there is nothing to argue about in the moment.
Losing streaks are a normal feature of this strategy. They are not a judgement on you.
Why it matters
Four, six, even ten losses in a row will happen inside a winning system.
What separates the traders who last from the ones who do not is how big they were trading when the streak arrived, and how quickly they cut back while it lasted.
The alarm
Several stop-outs in a row, or the feeling that you are giving profits back.
When that happens I stop trading and write up what happened before placing another order.
That feeling is the alarm. Trades taken just to feel like you are doing something are what keep a streak going.
The size ladder
As the month's drawdown deepens, I cut new-trade size in steps:
—
Down 3% — trade at three-quarter size.
—
Down 5% — half size.
—
Down 7% — quarter size.
—
Down 10% from peak equity — close everything, stand down, and spend a full day in the journal before the next trade.
No debate, no exceptions. The schedule decides so I do not have to.
The review
I pull up my last twenty trades and ask three things. Were the setups genuinely good, or stretched? Did I respect the limits, or argue with them? Had the market check already told me no?
The answers are always obvious in hindsight. The whole job of the protocol is to force that hindsight now.
How I run it
Streaks make you want to do more. The answer is always less: fewer names, smaller size, shorter sessions.
Tomorrow's size is set by last night's numbers, not by how this morning's market feels.
Size comes back the same way it left, one step at a time, as the equity curve earns it back. You cannot think your way back to even. You size your way back.
Common mistakes
Trading bigger to win it back. The one move that turns a drawdown into a hole.
Skipping the review day because the setups "look too good to miss".
Treating the size ladder as a suggestion.
Declaring the system broken at four losses when its own history says four losses are ordinary.
Takeaway
Cut size early and by the schedule, so you are still in good shape when conditions come back. Survival first — the recovery follows from it.

Progress over perfection
Repetitions add up; opinions don't
What this is
The most common way to fail with a system like this is not breaking the rules. It is freezing while following them.
The answer is simple. Take the trade the system flagged, at a size you can survive being wrong at, and let the number of correct repetitions do the work that waiting for certainty never will.
Why it matters
The edge in this book is small on any single trade. It only shows up over a long run of them.
The traders who compound are not the ones who were most often right. They are the ones who kept showing up, at survivable size, doing the same boring correct things.
What actually helps
—
Trade small when unsure. Shrinking the stakes of one decision cures most paralysis.
—
Write the trigger and the stop the night before. When the alert fires the decision is already made. You are just executing yesterday's clarity.
—
Log the trades you did not take beside the ones you did. That list teaches you faster than your profit and loss does. Most rejected setups were fine, and some rejected rules were right.
—
Judge the week on process, not on the result. Did you take the setups the system flagged? Did you cut what it said to cut? Did you respect the three limits? Three yeses is a good week, whatever the number says.
How I run it
Being unsure is a reason to trade smaller. It is not a reason to skip the day.
I do the deciding the night before, so the session is only execution.
And I grade the week on those three process questions before anyone looks at the profit and loss.
Common mistakes
Waiting for the perfect market, the perfect base and the perfect entry price while the leaders run without you — and the month ends flat with a notebook full of near-misses.
Treating a green week as proof of good process and a red one as proof of bad.
Only looking at your list of missed trades when it makes you feel better.
Takeaway
Done beats perfect. Take the trade the system flags, honour the stop, log it — and let the repetitions do what being clever cannot.

Your daily read
The card that does Part 1 and 2 for you
Situational awareness, written out every day
Pieces 01 to 03 ask you to check three things before you look at a chart: the market, the groups, and the rotation. That is real work, and doing it badly is worse than not doing it at all.
So I built this card into the app to do it for you. I write it every day, after the close, from the same numbers the pieces describe. You open it and the reading is already done.
The whole point is to make the market easier to navigate. You should not have to hold four different readings in your head before you have even opened a chart.
⤢
The card as you see it. One sentence at the top, three columns underneath, and a stance in the corner. Click to zoom.
How to read it
The stance
Top corner, colour-coded. Risk on means conditions favour buying, so trade the full playbook. Neutral means mixed, so half size and only the best setups. Risk off means breakouts are failing, so the best work you can do is build the watchlist. This one word sets your size for the day.
The headline
One or two sentences saying what actually happened. If you read nothing else, read this.
The weather
The market itself — piece 01. How many stocks rose against how many fell, and whether that agrees with what the index did.
The neighbourhood
Where the money is — pieces 02 and 03. Which groups lead, and the important part: whether they lead from their highs or are just bouncing off their lows.
What would change it
What I am watching for that would flip the stance. This is the one people skip, and it is the one that tells you what to do tomorrow.
How I use it
—
I read the stance first. That decides my size before I have looked at anything.
—
Then the headline, then the neighbourhood column. That tells me where to hunt tonight.
—
Then I open the charts. Never before.
Everything on the card is a reading, not a prediction. It describes what the market is doing right now. It does not tell you what happens next, and neither does anything else in this book.
Use it as the first thing you check, not the last thing you argue with.

My chart
Tools I use on TradingView
Six indicators, and what each one is for
These are the six things on my chart, and nothing is there for decoration. Each one answers a question this book already asks: how far away is the stop, is this stock outperforming, does it move enough to be worth trading, is anyone actually here this morning, and is the trend still intact. Add them once and you stop guessing. Every link below opens the script on TradingView.
What the finished chart looks like. Three panels: the strength line on top, price with the 10, 20, 50 and 200-day lines in the middle, and volume at the bottom. Set it up once, then use the same layout on every stock.
01
ADR% / ATR / LoD dist. Table
A small table on the chart showing three numbers: how far the stock moves in a normal day (ADR%), its average true range, and how far price is right now from the low of the day.
This is the entry check, made visible. Our stop is the low of the day, so the distance to it is our risk — and it has to stay inside roughly one normal day's range. Before clicking buy, this table answers the only two questions that decide the size: how far away is the stop, and is that distance sensible for this stock. If the low of the day is already far below when the trigger fires, the trade does not fit. Pass it, or take fewer shares.
tradingview.com/script/6A822fQ5-ADR-ATR-LoD-dist-Table/
02
TraderLion's Relative Strength Line
A line under the price chart showing the stock's performance against the index, with a mark whenever that line hits a new high — including when it does so before the stock does.
This is how we check relative strength. What we want: the line making higher highs while the market chops, and a new high on the line before the stock breaks out — that is buying showing up early. What kills a candidate: the line rolling over while the price still looks fine, or dropping to new lows on a bounce day. Its most useful moment is when the index is making new lows: a stock holding a higher low right then is the signature of the next leader.
tradingview.com/script/N4Iqr5Cz-TraderLion-s-Relative-Strength-Line/
03
Swing Data — ADR% / RVol / PVol / Float / Avg Vol
One panel with the numbers that decide whether a stock is worth trading: how much it moves in a day, today's volume against normal, pre-market volume, shares available (float), and average volume.
The checklist on the chart instead of in a spreadsheet. The stock has to move enough to pay for its stop — one that barely travels never will. Average volume and float tell you whether you can get in and out at size, and whether the shares are scarce enough for buying to really move the price. Pre-market volume is the first honest read on a gapper before the bell: real interest turns up early, and a gap with nothing behind it usually sells.
tradingview.com/script/uloAa2EI-Swing-Data-ADR-RVol-PVol-Float-Avg-Vol/
04
IBD Style Relative Volume — Intraday Adjusted
Today's volume compared against the same time of day on a normal day, instead of against a whole day's total.
This is what makes the volume check mean anything. At 9:45 a stock has only traded a fraction of its day, so comparing that to a full day's average always looks low and tells you nothing. Measured against the same time on normal days, it tells you whether this morning is unusual. The rule is simple: a trigger on its own is not enough until the volume confirms it. Once you are holding, switch back to the daily reading against the 50-day average — one for the entry, the other for the hold.
tradingview.com/script/p9PqBCAX-IBD-Style-Relative-Volume-Intraday-Adjusted/
05
ATR% Multiple From MA
One number telling you how far price sits above its 50-day line, measured in the stock's own normal daily moves instead of in plain percent.
This is my profit-taking gauge, and it is the honest way to answer “is this stock extended?” Plain percent cannot answer it: two stocks both 10% above the 50-day line are in completely different situations if one normally moves 1% a day and the other moves 7%. Dividing the distance by the stock's normal daily range fixes that and puts every stock on one scale. Under 4 is the launchpad — price is near its 50-day line, bases form here, and this is where I am allowed to buy. Around 5 no adds, start tightening. 7.5 to 8 is rare air and my trims scale up. Around 10 on a single stock, sell aggressively into the move — but on an index or a leveraged fund the same extreme arrives at 6 to 7, because a basket of stocks never stretches as far as one. A high reading is a trim signal, never a short signal.
tradingview.com/script/oimVgV7e-ATR-multiple-from-50-MA/
06
Moving Average Ribbon
TradingView's built-in ribbon, set to our own periods: SMA 10 / 20 / 50 / 200 on daily charts, and EMA 10 / 20 / 65 on intraday charts.
The lines the trend runs along. On the daily chart they show whether the trend is healthy and give every base its shape — price riding the rising 10 and 20 into a pivot, the 50 as the line a leader defends, the 200 as the difference between an uptrend and a repair job. When the short lines come together at a pivot, the stock is coiling. On intraday charts the faster set does the same job inside the day: while price holds above them the day is intact, and losing them is the first sign it is not. One ribbon, two timeframes, the same question — is this still in gear?

The card
The one-page card
Twenty-one pieces, compressed to what you check before the click.
The three scans
Top gainers over 1M/3M/6M (with liquidity and volatility floors) · CANSLIM scanner (price >$3 · cap >$300M · revenue qtrly YoY >25% · avg vol 60d >500K · price above the 50-day · volatility >3% · float <150M · ≥70% above the 52-week low) · pre-market gappers, the scan built for episodic pivots.
The five setups
Volatility contraction pattern (VCP) · high tight flag (HTF) · undercut and rally (UnR) · episodic pivot (EP) · parabolic short / breakdown (PS) — the short side only after a top is already in.
Sizing
Shares = (Account × Risk%) ÷ (Entry − Stop) · risk ¼–⅓% per trade · total open risk ≤ ~2% · money held overnight ≤ ~30%, counting break-even positions.
What a candidate needs
Group in the top five · strong relative strength over 1, 3 and 6 months · enough volume to trade at size · moves enough to pay for its stop · fresh, not stretched above the 50-day.
Entry
5-minute opening-range high · time-matched relative volume confirming · stop = low of day, ≤ ~1 daily range, placed with the order.
Managing the trade
At +3R–5R (or day 3–5 as the fallback): sell 30–50%, move the stop to break-even · when it stretches far above the 50-day, sell into the move — never short it · trail the rest on one moving average, daily closes only, forgiving quick dips · add only at a new pause, with the stop moving up.
No-buy — any yes kills it
Market check red? · Group not leading, or only bouncing off its lows? · Stretched? · Loose base? · No volume on the break? · Earnings within 5 days? · Open risk already at the cap? · Trading a feeling?
Survival
Run of losses, or giving profits back → stop and review · cut size in steps as the month draws down · at the hard line → close everything, stand down, spend a day in the journal.
